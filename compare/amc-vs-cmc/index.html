AMC vs CMC: what each contract covers, and who carries the spare-parts risk.

AMC and CMC sound like the same product with a different letter. They are two different risk positions. This page sets out what each covers, how the pricing logic follows from that, and where warranty and extended warranty sit alongside them.

Who each is for

Labour only, or labour plus parts

An AMC, an annual maintenance contract, is for the buyer who wants scheduled servicing and a guaranteed response, and is willing to pay separately for whatever part fails. It suits reliable equipment, a buyer with a spares budget, and a dealer who does not want to underwrite an unpredictable parts bill.

A CMC, a comprehensive maintenance contract, is for the buyer who wants one predictable annual figure covering labour and the parts fitted during the year. It suits critical equipment where downtime is expensive, buyers who need a fixed line in the budget, and dealers who know the failure pattern of that model well enough to price the risk. The letter that changes is small; the risk that moves is not.

How each works

Definitions, and what actually sits inside each contract

AMC, annual maintenance contract

The dealer commits to a defined number of preventive maintenance visits in the year, plus breakdown attendance within an agreed response time. Engineer time and travel are included. Spare parts, consumables and often calibration are billed separately when used, at a rate card attached to the contract. The customer's annual outlay is the contract value plus whatever breaks.

CMC, comprehensive maintenance contract

The same visits and response commitments, with spare parts included. The contract normally lists exclusions, and those exclusions are the whole negotiation: consumables, probes, batteries, detectors, tubes, glassware, accidental damage, damage from power fluctuation, and anything classified as a major assembly. A CMC without a clear exclusion list is a dispute waiting to happen.

Warranty and extended warranty, the other two options

Warranty is the manufacturer's or dealer's obligation included in the purchase price, running for a defined period from installation. It typically covers defects rather than wear, and it is the reason a service contract should start the day the warranty ends, not the day someone remembers. Extended warranty continues those original terms for a further period and is bought before the original expires. Because it inherits the warranty's defect wording it is usually narrower than a CMC, and it rarely includes preventive visits.

How to decide

Price the risk before you name the letter

For a dealer, the decision is underwriting. A CMC is profitable when you have enough service history on that model to know roughly what it consumes in a year, and dangerous when you do not. That history exists only if every job card, part issued and breakdown was recorded against the serial number. Sell CMC on the equipment you know and AMC on the equipment you are still learning.

  • Offer AMC when the parts risk is genuinely unpredictable, when the customer has its own biomedical team and a spares budget, or when the equipment is simple enough that labour is the whole job.
  • Offer CMC when downtime is expensive for the customer, when you have a reliable spare-parts supply chain for that model, and when you can write an exclusion list that both sides understand at signing.
  • Offer extended warranty when the customer wants continuity of the original terms rather than a service programme, and pair it with a preventive maintenance schedule so the machine is still serviced.

For a hospital buying, the question is which risk you would rather hold. AMC keeps the annual fee lower and leaves you exposed to one bad failure; CMC converts that exposure into a known figure. Ask for the exclusion list, the response time and the number of preventive visits in writing before comparing quotations.

Whichever you run, the operational risk is the same: contracts lapse quietly, and a renewal nobody chased is revenue gone. Questions

AMC vs CMC, answered

How is a CMC different from an AMC?

An AMC covers preventive visits, breakdown attendance and labour, with spare parts billed separately. A CMC covers the same service plus the spare parts fitted during the year, subject to a stated exclusion list. The difference is who carries the parts risk.

Is CMC always more expensive than AMC?

The contract value is higher because it includes parts. Total annual cost to the buyer can be lower if a significant part fails, and higher if nothing does. That is the trade: a fixed figure against a variable one.

What is usually excluded from a CMC?

Consumables and wear items are the common exclusions, along with accidental damage, damage from power fluctuation, and sometimes named major assemblies. The exclusion list should be attached to the contract and read before signing, not after a failure.

When should a service contract start?

The day the warranty ends. Gaps between warranty expiry and contract start are where unbilled service happens. Tracking warranty end dates against the installed base is the only reliable way to catch them. See warranty management.
