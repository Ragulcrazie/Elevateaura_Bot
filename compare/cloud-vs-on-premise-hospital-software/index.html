Cloud vs on-premise hospital software: what happens when the line goes down.

Where your hospital system runs decides who maintains it, where the records sit, what the front desk can do during an outage and how you pay for it. This page sets out the trade-offs so you can ask the right questions before you sign.

Who each is for

A server in your building, or a workspace you sign in to

On-premise means the hospital system runs on a machine inside your hospital. Staff reach it over the local network, so the front desk keeps working when the internet does not. In exchange, the server, its power, its backups and its replacement in five years are yours to look after, and remote access is something you have to arrange deliberately.

Cloud means the system runs on managed infrastructure and every counter signs in over the internet. Nobody in the hospital patches a server or worries about a failed disk, a doctor can open the record from home, and new modules arrive without anyone visiting. In exchange, the hospital depends on its connection, and the data sits somewhere you should ask about in writing.

Most Indian hospitals choose on the strength of one question: what does the registration counter do at eleven in the morning when the line is down.

What actually differs

Four things that change, and one that does not

Internet dependence

This is the real difference and the one buyers underestimate. On-premise, a connection failure stops remote viewing and outside communication, while registration, prescribing, dispensing and billing carry on across the local network. On cloud, a connection failure stops the counters. The honest mitigation is not a promise, it is a second connection from a different provider, and someone whose job it is to notice when the first one fails.

Who maintains it

On-premise, the hospital owns operating system updates, antivirus, the backup job and the hardware. Small hospitals often discover the backup was failing quietly for months. On cloud, that work sits with the provider and the hospital never sees it, which is a genuine saving in a building with no IT staff.

Where the data sits

On-premise, the records are physically in your hospital, which is reassuring, and exactly as safe as the room the machine is in and the state of your backups. On cloud, the records sit in a data centre. Ask where, ask what the backup and retention arrangements are, ask how you get a full export if you leave, and get the answers in the agreement. Regulatory obligations on health data change, so confirm your position with your own advisers rather than relying on a vendor's summary.

How you pay

Without quoting figures, the shapes differ. On-premise tends to front-load: hardware and a licence at the start, then support. Cloud tends to spread the same money across a subscription with the infrastructure inside it. The comparison is only meaningful over several years and only when hardware replacement, power, backup and someone's time are counted on the on-premise side.

How to decide

Answer these before you choose

The deployment question is worth settling before the software question, because it changes what you should be demonstrating.

  • How often does your connection actually fail, and for how long? Ask the staff, not the provider. If the answer is weekly, cloud-only needs a second line.
  • Is there anyone who could look after a server? If nobody in the building can tell you when the last backup ran, on-premise has a hidden cost.
  • Do doctors need the record from outside? Remote reading and after-hours access are simplest on cloud.
  • Will you add a second branch? One workspace across sites is much easier than servers in two buildings.
  • What must never leave the building? If that list is long, say so early; it changes the design, not just the price.
  • What happens if you leave? Agree the export format and timeline in the contract while you are still friendly.

Questions

Cloud vs on-premise, answered

What happens to a cloud hospital system when the internet fails?

The counters stop until the connection returns, because every screen is served over it. Hospitals that choose cloud in areas with unreliable connectivity usually take a second line from a different provider and make one person responsible for noticing a failure quickly.

Is on-premise more secure than cloud?

Not automatically. On-premise data is as secure as the room, the machine, the passwords and the backups around it. Cloud shifts that work to a provider with dedicated staff. What matters in both cases is role-based access, audit trails and tested backups.

How is Elevate Aura's HIMS deployed?

Can we start on cloud and move on-premise later, or the other way round?

Treat it as a change of deployment rather than a setting, because it affects access, backups and support. Raise it as a requirement before signing so the scope reflects it, instead of discovering the position after go-live.

Which costs less over five years?

It depends on your hardware, connectivity and staff. On-premise front-loads the spend and adds hardware renewal, power and someone's time. Cloud spreads it monthly with infrastructure included. Elevate Aura scopes each project on a call rather than publishing a price list.

Where will our patient data be stored?

That is a question to answer in the agreement, covering location, backups, retention and how you obtain a full export if you leave. Health data obligations change over time, so confirm your own position with your advisers.
