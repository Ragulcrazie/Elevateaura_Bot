CRM vs ERP for a medical equipment distributor: and the gap between them.

A CRM wins the order. An ERP accounts for the company. A distributor that installs and services equipment lives mostly in the space between the two, which is where contracts lapse and service calls go missing. This page maps that space honestly.

Who each is for

One wins the order. One accounts for the company.

A CRM is for the part of the business still trying to win an order: leads, hospital and clinic accounts, decision-makers, quotations and follow-up. It ends more or less at the purchase order. An ERP is for the part that has to account for everything afterwards: procurement, stock, ledgers, payables, payroll and statutory filing, usually across several branches, warehouses or legal entities.

A medical-equipment distributor sits between the two, because the recurring money is in what happens after delivery: installation, warranty, the AMC or CMC contract, the renewal, the breakdown call, the spare part consumed on that call. A plain CRM does not track a service contract and a classic ERP does not dispatch a technician. That is the gap an operations platform such as Aura Business is built to close.

How each works

What sits inside each kind of system

What a CRM covers

A CRM holds the pipeline. Enquiries arrive, a rep is assigned, the account and its decision-makers are recorded, a quotation goes out, follow-ups are logged and the deal is marked won or lost. Good ones add territory and target management and conversion analytics, so an owner can see which source and which rep produce orders. What a CRM does not do is carry a serial number forward, know that a machine is under warranty until a certain date, or tell you that its contract lapses next month.

What an ERP covers

An ERP is an accounting and resource backbone: purchase orders and goods receipt, stock valuation, the general ledger, receivables and payables, payroll, fixed assets and consolidation across entities. It is designed so every transaction lands in the books correctly, which is what you need if you manufacture, import at scale, or run several companies. It is heavy by design, and implementing one is a project rather than a purchase.

Where the after-sales loop falls

Between the two sits the loop that decides whether a distributor is profitable in year three: the installed-base register of every unit sold, warranty tracking, AMC and CMC contracts with their renewal dates, preventive maintenance schedules, breakdown intake, technician dispatch, the mobile job card with photo proof, the spare part that drops out of stock when it is used, and the service invoice that follows. Most distributors run that loop on WhatsApp and a spreadsheet, because neither system they bought covers it.

How to decide

Decide by where the work actually goes

Spend a week noticing where your team's hours go. If almost all of it is chasing enquiries and sending quotations, a CRM is enough. If a large part of it is receiving imports, valuing stock and closing books across companies, an ERP earns its cost. If most hours go on service calls, renewals and figuring out which machine is where, neither is the system you need.

  • A full ERP is genuinely right when you manufacture or assemble, when you have more than one legal entity to consolidate, when landed-cost allocation matters, or when a statutory audit drives your reporting calendar. Those are the reasons ERPs exist.
  • A CRM alone is right when you sell and hand over, with no installation, no warranty obligation and no service contract. A pure sales team does not need a service module.
  • An operations platform is right when the same customer comes back for service for years. See distributor software and AMC and CMC software.

There is also a sensible middle path: keep the accounting package your chartered accountant knows, run operations on a platform that covers sale through service, and export between the two. Aura Business is built that way, with GST-native tax invoices and e-way bills at one end and an accounting export at the other.

Whichever way you go, ask any vendor to demonstrate one path end to end on your own data: an enquiry becoming a quotation, an invoice, an installed asset, a contract with a renewal date, and a service call with a job card.

Questions

CRM vs ERP for distributors, answered

What is the actual difference between a CRM and an ERP?

A CRM manages relationships and the pipeline before an order. An ERP manages resources and accounts after it: stock, ledgers, payables, payroll. They overlap at the invoice, which is why many businesses end up owning both.

Does a medical equipment distributor need an ERP?

Only if you manufacture, import at scale, or consolidate several legal entities. A trading and service distributor needs strong GST billing, stock and an after-sales system, which an operations platform covers without an ERP project.

Can a CRM handle AMC renewals?

Most cannot. A CRM tracks opportunities, not assets under contract. AMC and CMC need an installed-base register, contract dates, preventive maintenance schedules and a renewal engine. See what AMC software is.

Where does GST billing belong?

Wherever the order is raised, so the invoice matches the quotation and the stock movement without re-keying. Aura Business raises the tax invoice, challan, e-invoice and e-way bill alongside the order, then exports to your accounting package.

Is an operations platform just a CRM with extra modules?

The difference is the installed base. A CRM ends at the order; an operations platform keeps a record of every unit delivered, its warranty, contract and service history, and drives dispatch and renewal from it.
