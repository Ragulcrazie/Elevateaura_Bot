EMR vs EHR: one record inside your hospital, one that follows the patient out of it.

An EMR is the clinical record your hospital keeps about a patient. An EHR is a record that travels with the patient across the places that treat them. This guide explains the difference and what an Indian hospital actually needs to buy.

The short answer

The difference in two paragraphs

An EMR, or electronic medical record, is the clinical record one provider keeps about a patient: history, examination, diagnosis, orders, progress notes, prescriptions and reports, held inside that hospital or clinic. It replaces the paper case sheet and it belongs to the institution that wrote it.

An EHR, or electronic health record, is the wider idea: a record that follows the patient across every provider who treats them, so a hospital can see what a different clinic did last year. The distinction is not the software's features. It is whether the record stops at your boundary or crosses it.

Where the confusion comes from

Why the two words are used interchangeably

Most products sold as EHRs are EMRs with a sharing feature, and most buyers do not need to care until the day they need a record from somewhere else.

The terms were separated in American policy language, where EHR implied interoperability with other providers and EMR did not. Indian vendors inherited the vocabulary without the distinction, so the two words now appear on the same brochure. A useful test cuts through it: ask who else can read this record, under what consent, and by what standard. If the answer is only staff inside this hospital, it is an EMR, whatever the brochure says.

What an EMR gives you

Legibility, retrieval and continuity inside the building. The consultant sees the last visit, the ward nurse sees what the OP doctor ordered, the pharmacy dispenses against the prescription rather than a handwritten slip, and the bill is drawn from what was actually done. That alone removes most of the daily damage caused by paper.

What an EHR adds

Continuity outside the building: the discharge summary from another hospital, the scan done at a diagnostic centre two streets away, the prescription written by a clinic in another district. That requires identity, consent and a shared standard, which is why it is a national infrastructure question rather than a software feature you can simply switch on.

The Indian picture

Where ABDM and ABHA fit

India's answer to the EHR question is the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission. ABDM does not ask hospitals to hand their records to a central database. It defines an identity for the patient, the ABHA number, and a consent-based way for records held by different providers to be discovered and shared when the patient allows it. The record stays where it was written; what moves is a pointer and a permission.

  • ABHA number. The patient's health identifier, used to link records held in different places to one person.
  • Consent. Sharing is patient-led and time-bound rather than open access between institutions.
  • Records stay with the provider. Your hospital keeps what it wrote; it is made discoverable, not surrendered.
  • Structure matters. A record kept as scanned images cannot be shared usefully. Structured fields can.

What a hospital should ask a vendor is narrow and answerable: can you link an ABHA number to our patient record, is our documentation structured rather than free text, and what is your path as the standards develop. In HIMS the record is structured, supports NABH-style documentation and is ABHA and ABDM ready. That is a statement about the software, not a certification of your hospital; accreditation and registration remain your process, and you should confirm your obligations with your own advisers.

Practically, the order of work is the same for almost every hospital. Get a structured EMR running on one patient identifier first, because an EHR built on duplicate records and scanned paper shares confusion rather than care. See hospital EMR software and ABHA and ABDM ready software.

Questions

EMR and EHR, answered

What is the difference between EMR and EHR?

An EMR is the clinical record a single hospital or clinic keeps about a patient. An EHR is a record that follows the patient across providers. The difference is reach and sharing, not the quality of the notes inside.

Which does a small hospital or clinic need?

A structured EMR on one patient identifier, inside a system that also handles registration, pharmacy, laboratory and billing. Cross-provider sharing becomes useful afterwards, and it is much easier to add once your own record is clean.

Is an EMR the same as a hospital management system?

No. The EMR is the clinical half. A hospital management system adds registration, appointments, pharmacy, laboratory, beds, theatre, billing and claims around it. Buying an EMR alone leaves the counters running on paper.

Does ABDM make my hospital an EHR?

ABDM provides the identity and consent layer that lets records held by different providers be shared with the patient's permission. Your hospital still keeps its own record. Being ABDM ready means the software can participate, not that your records have been centralised.

Do scanned documents count as an electronic record?

They are better than a lost file and worse than structured data. A scan cannot be searched, charted or shared field by field. Structured entry is what lets a nurse see today's orders and lets a record be exchanged meaningfully later.

Who owns the patient record?

In practice the provider that created it holds it, while the patient has rights over their health information and over consent to share it. The specifics depend on current regulation and your own contracts, so confirm the position with your advisers.
