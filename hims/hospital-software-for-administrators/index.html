What the administrator's job needs from a system

An administrator does not use a hospital system the way a doctor or a billing clerk does. They are not entering data, they are answering for it. How many beds are free in the male ward, why did the pharmacy counter have a queue at noon, what has been collected today, who is on duty tomorrow evening, and who changed that bill. Those questions arrive in no particular order and usually need an answer before the next one arrives.

HIMS is useful here because every one of those answers comes from work the hospital has already done. Occupancy comes from bed management, collections from billing, departmental activity from the record the departments write to, rosters from the doctor schedule. Nothing is compiled, and because entries are attributed and timed, an administrator can go from a figure on the dashboard to the entry that produced it.

That last point matters more than it sounds. An administrator spends much of the day being told things by people who are busy, and a figure that can be opened down to the bill, the admission or the entry behind it is the difference between managing and mediating.

The administrator's normal day

Most of the job is finding out what already happened.

Not because it is hidden, but because it is in four places and three of them are people.

Occupancy by phone call

Collections known tomorrow

Department performance by anecdote

Which department is busy is a matter of opinion because nobody produces comparable numbers.

Rosters on a printed sheet

Who is sitting when is a notice on a wall, and changes are communicated verbally.

No trail behind a change

A bill was altered or a discharge was delayed and there is no record of who did what, when.

In practice

Four questions answered without leaving the chair.

Can we take this admission?

Workflow: The ward view shows free beds by category before the answer is given to the referring doctor.

Result: The bed is promised on fact.

Why is the pharmacy queue long?

Workflow: The dispensing queue and the OP token counts for the morning are read side by side.

Result: Staffing is moved where the load is.

A family disputes a bill

Workflow: The bill is opened with its itemised charges and the entries behind them, including who recorded what.

Result: The conversation happens with evidence.

A consultant wants an extra clinic

Workflow: OP volume for that doctor across the last period is read before the roster is changed.

Result: The decision is not made on impressions.

Questions

For administrators, answered.

Can I see occupancy without calling the wards?

Yes. Bed management shows every ward and bed as it currently stands, updated by the admissions, transfers and discharges the wards are already recording. See bed management.

Can I control what each staff member can see?

Access is role based, so front desk, nursing, pharmacy, billing and management each see what their role allows. The roles are configured at setup and changed as your team changes.

Is there an audit trail behind a bill or a record?

Entries are attributed to the user and timed, and bills carry an audit trail, so a disputed charge or an altered record can be traced rather than argued about.

Does it handle doctor rosters?

Doctor rosters and consulting days are held in the system and drive the appointment book and the token queues, so a change to who is sitting when reaches the front desk rather than a notice board.

How is this different from the owner's view?

The owner wants the headline daily. An administrator needs to act on it during the day and reach the entry behind it. Same platform, different depth. See the owner dashboard.

How long before we are running on it?

The scope is fixed at the start and the target is live in 30 working days where that scope allows, including migration of your existing data and training at your own counters.
