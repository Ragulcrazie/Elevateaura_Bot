The discharge gap

Hospitals send patients home before families are ready for the care.

Discharge does not mean recovered. It means the rest of the healing happens at home, often with instructions no one at home is trained to follow.

Wounds and drains

Surgical sites and drains need clean, correct care or they get infected.

Confusing medication

New tablets, changed doses and injections that families struggle to manage.

Warning signs missed

Fever, swelling or a leaking wound go unnoticed until it is an emergency.

Weak and immobile

A post-surgery patient cannot move safely without support.

Readmission risk

Small problems handled late send patients straight back to hospital.

Anxious first nights

The family watches nervously with no clinical eyes on the patient.

Questions after discharge

Post-hospital care, answered.

When should post-hospital care start?

Ideally from the day of discharge. The first days home are when wounds, medication and mobility carry the most risk, so early nurse support helps recovery and lowers readmission risk.

Can you follow the discharge summary?

Yes. Share the discharge summary and prescriptions. The nurse works to the doctor's instructions for dressings, medication and follow-up.

Do you handle surgical drains and stitches?

Yes. Drain observation, dressing changes and suture removal on the doctor's timeline are all part of post-hospital home care.

How long is care usually needed?

It depends on the surgery or illness. Many families use daily or alternate-day visits for one to three weeks, then reduce as recovery progresses.

Do you offer overnight monitoring?

Yes. For the first risky nights, see 12 and 24 hour home nursing.
