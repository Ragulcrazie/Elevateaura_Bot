What a diagnostic equipment supplier actually sells

A diagnostic equipment supplier rarely makes its living on the analyser. The instrument goes into a pathology lab, a hospital lab or a collection centre, and what follows it is a stream of reagents, controls, calibrators, cuvettes and rotors that only you supply. Software for this trade has to connect the placed machine to that stream, so every analyser on your books shows the consumables it should be consuming and whether the lab is actually ordering them.

The second half of the job is uptime. A lab with a dead analyser cannot report samples, so it sends the work elsewhere and stops ordering your reagents that week. Aura Business keeps the asset register, the calibration and quality control visit schedule, the breakdown call clock and the consumable order history on the same record, so a drop in reagent orders and a rise in service calls are visible as the connected problem they usually are.

What goes wrong

The analyser is placed. Then nobody watches what it eats.

Placement decisions are made on a consumption forecast. Very few suppliers ever check the forecast against reality on a specific machine.

Pull-through unmeasured

An analyser was placed on the promise of monthly reagent volume, and nobody can say whether that lab has ordered any of it since March.

Reagent expiry written off

Cold-chain stock is ordered in a large lot, sits past its date in the warehouse and is scrapped, because batch dates live on cartons rather than in a system.

Calibration visits slip

Each instrument needs periodic calibration and QC verification. The schedule exists in an engineer's diary, so it runs late until the lab complains.

Breakdowns answered by chance

A call comes to a mobile number. Whether an engineer reaches the lab today depends on who picked up, not on what the contract promised.

Consumable orders re-keyed

The same lab sends the same reagent list by WhatsApp every month and someone types it into an invoice every month.

No view of a lab's worth

The instrument, the contract, the reagent orders and the service cost sit in four places, so nobody knows which placements pay.

In practice

Four moments that decide a reagent business.

A placement goes quiet

Workflow: The analyser record shows no consumable order for two cycles while two breakdown calls were logged in the same period.

Result: The area manager visits before the lab moves the work to another supplier.

A short-dated lot is used first

Workflow: Batch and expiry tracking surfaces a reagent lot nearing its date, and despatch picks that lot for the next order to a high-volume lab.

Result: Stock is sold rather than scrapped.

A calibration visit lands on time

Workflow: The preventive schedule raises the calibration and quality control visit, the engineer completes the checklist on the mobile job card and captures the operator's signature.

Result: The lab has the record its own quality review asks for.

A lab orders without ringing anyone

Workflow: The lab manager opens the portal, repeats last month's reagent list, and the order arrives priced and ready for despatch.

Result: No re-keying, and an order history that can be forecast against.

Questions

Diagnostic equipment supply, answered.

Can we see reagent consumption against a specific analyser?

Yes. The instrument is an asset with a serial number and a site, and the consumables that run on it are linked to it, so the record shows what that laboratory has ordered and when the last order was placed.

How is reagent expiry handled?

Every lot is received with its batch number and expiry date, stock is held by batch, and alerts surface lots approaching their date. Despatch can pick the oldest usable lot, and returns of short-dated stock are recorded with a credit note.

Can calibration and quality control visits be scheduled separately from ordinary service?

Yes. Preventive maintenance schedules carry their own calibration and compliance checklists, so a calibration visit raises its own job with its own checklist rather than being folded into a breakdown call.

Does the system track how fast we reach a lab after a breakdown call?

Yes. The service call is logged at intake, dispatch and completion are stamped on the job, and SLA definitions on the contract show whether the response commitment was met. See SLA management software.

Can a laboratory place its own repeat orders?

Yes. The customer self-service portal lets the lab repeat a previous order at its agreed prices and follow the despatch on an order tracking page. Larger suppliers give sub-dealers the distributor order portal instead.

Can we tell whether a placement is profitable?

Contract profitability compares the contract value and reagent revenue on an instrument against the visits and parts consumed on it. That is the number that tells you whether a class of placement should be repeated.
